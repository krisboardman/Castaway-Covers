# Motor

## What is on the towel [observed 2026-10-23]

- A small black-enamelled motor. Its laminated field core and coil are exposed in
  the middle of the body.
- One end housing has a round slotted cap on the side (very likely a carbon brush
  holder; expect a matching one opposite) and a small brass tube (oil or grease
  point) [inferred].
- A two-strand, cloth-covered cord ending in two ring terminals. The cloth is
  frayed near the ends.
- A black L-shaped bracket: almost certainly the mount that holds the motor
  against the machine so its pulley drives the belt [inferred].
- No maker's plate is visible from the camera angle. **Find it**: the model
  letters, volts and amps decide which parts fit. Singer 66 variants were sold
  with BR, Series 4 and BY motors [source 3]; others were fitted later, and
  refinished machines often carry non-Singer motor kits [source 4].

## Safety first

- Never plug it in to "see if it works" until the cord is checked. On motors this
  age the rubber under the cloth crumbles; bare conductors inside intact-looking
  cloth are common [inferred].
- Unplug before touching anything. Old foot controllers can short internally too.

## Service sequence

Work in this order; each step can stop the job if it finds a dead part.

1. **Identify.** Read the data plate. Photograph it with `camera_save_photo`.
2. **Cord.** Flex each lead along its length. Cracking, stiffness or bare copper
   means replace both leads (pre-terminated lead sets are sold [source 7]). Use a
   meter for continuity and for no short between leads and the motor body.
3. **Brushes.** Unscrew the caps, lift out spring and brush. Note which way each
   brush faces and put it back the same way. Replace if worn short or the face is
   chipped or uneven; Vintage Singer Parts uses under 1/4" as the replace point
   for Singer motor brushes [source 8]. Brush sizes vary by motor: measure the
   cross-section before ordering [source 9].
4. **Commutator.** Clean with a dry, clean cloth; a rubber eraser only if it
   needs brightening. This is Singer's own instruction for its motors [source 5].
   Do not use emery cloth (it embeds grit). If armature wires are broken, bare or
   burned, the motor needs rewinding or replacing [source 5].
5. **Lubrication.** Singer's rule for its motors: grease, never oil, in the
   motor's lubrication points [source 5]. Light grease is the usual advice for
   belt motors too [source 9]. Clean old hardened grease out first; replace felt
   wicks if fitted [source 8].
6. **Spin test by hand.** The shaft should turn freely with no grinding or
   tight spots.
7. **Refit.** Bolt the bracket to the machine, fit the belt, and set the motor
   so the belt is snug but not tight. Turn the handwheel by hand once before
   powering up [inferred].
8. **First power-up.** Low speed, short bursts, watch for sparking at the brushes.
   A little sparking is normal; a ring of fire round the commutator is not.

## If it turns out to be a 15-91 potted motor after all

Kept short because the serial says otherwise ([identity.md](identity.md)). From the
15-91 adjusters manual [source 5]: remove two thumb screws, clean the grease cups
and fill with Singer motor lubricant, about one-quarter tube per cup; never oil.
Wicks: remove cap screws, take out retainers, springs and wicks; refit with the
wick ends against the armature shaft. The gear is a Textolite gear in the balance
wheel with a shock-absorbing spring.
